How to take a break from social media without losing your friends

Most people who want a break from social media don't want to disappear. They want to stop scrolling, not stop hearing from the people they care about. Here's how to do one without the other, and what the research says you can expect.

By Unreeled · Updated {{modified}}

The short version

  • Quit the feed, not your friends. Reels, Explore and suggested posts are what keep you scrolling. Messages and your friends' stories can stay.
  • Start with a week. In randomised trials, a single week off social media was enough to improve people's wellbeing and ease depression and anxiety.
  • Tell people how to reach you before you go quiet.
  • Change your phone, not just your willpower. Take the app off your home screen, switch off its notifications, and put a pause between you and the feed.
  • Plan for the moments you'd normally scroll, and decide how you'll come back before you do.

Why a break helps

When researchers ask people, chosen at random, to quit or cut back on social media for one to four weeks, those people usually end up slightly happier and less anxious or depressed than people who carried on as normal (Tromholt, 2016; Lambert et al., 2022). An analysis of 32 such trials found small but consistent improvements in depression, anxiety, stress and wellbeing (Burnell et al., 2025). A break won't change your life on its own, but the effect is real.

The time adds up, too. Facebook users paid to deactivate their accounts for four weeks gained about an hour a day, and spent more of it offline, including with friends and family (Allcott et al., 2020).

And if stopping feels harder than it should, that's not a character flaw. Feeds are built to be hard to put down: endless, unpredictable and tuned to you. Economists estimate that self-control problems account for about a third of the time people spend on social media and similar apps (Allcott, Gentzkow & Song, 2022). 1. Decide what you're quitting

Instagram, TikTok and Facebook bundle two very different things:

  • Your people: messages, group chats, your friends' stories and posts, invitations.
  • The feed: Reels, Explore, suggested posts and ads, picked by an algorithm to keep you watching, and more and more of it from accounts you don't follow. On Instagram, more than half of what people see is now recommended by Meta's AI (Meta, 2024).

For most people the feed is the problem and the people are the point. So a break doesn't have to be all or nothing. Pick the kind that fits:

Kind of breakWhat you keepGood for
A full breakNothing: apps deleted, accounts logged out or deactivatedA reset, to see what a week or a month without it is like
Messages onlyDirect messages and group chatsStaying reachable while the scrolling habit fades
Friends onlyYour friends' posts and stories, and messages, without Reels, Explore or suggestionsThe long run: staying in touch without the rabbit hole

2. Choose how long

There's no magic number, but the experiments give a guide:

  • One week was enough to improve wellbeing in two trials, one with 1,095 Facebook users and one with 154 adults who left Facebook, Instagram, Twitter and TikTok (Tromholt, 2016; Lambert et al., 2022).
  • Three to four weeks gives the habit more time to fade. Students who limited Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes each a day for three weeks felt less lonely and depressed (Hunt et al., 2018), and Facebook users who deactivated for four weeks said they used it less afterwards (Allcott et al., 2020).
  • Cutting down counts. Across trials, cutting back worked about as well as quitting completely, and longer breaks didn't reliably do better (Burnell et al., 2025; May et al., 2025). Students with anxiety or depression who kept to an hour a day for three weeks felt better about their appearance and weight (Thai et al., 2024).

Put the end date in your calendar. Coming back then is a decision rather than a relapse, and you can always extend it.

3. Tell people how to reach you

The fear of missing out is mostly a fear of missing people. Deal with it before you start:

  • Post a story or a short note saying you're taking a break, and how to reach you.
  • Message the handful of people you talk to most, so they know why you've gone quiet.
  • Ask a friend to pass on invitations to anything that's only organised on Instagram or Facebook.
  • Decide which group chats you'll keep. Keeping messages while you drop the feed is still a real break.

Something like: Taking a break from Instagram for a few weeks. I'm still around: text me, or message me here.

4. Change your phone, not just your willpower

Promising yourself to scroll less pits willpower against software tuned on billions of people. Changing the setup works better. In one trial, students who followed a list of such changes, from moving social apps off the home screen to switching to grayscale, cut their screen time by nearly an hour a day, against about ten minutes in the comparison group (Olson et al., 2022).

Take the app off your phone

Deleting an app doesn't delete your account, and you can reinstall it in a minute. On iPhone, touch and hold the app, then Remove App › Delete App. If that feels like too much, choose Remove from Home Screen instead: the app stays in the App Library, one search away, but out of reach of muscle memory.

Switch off the feed's notifications

Notifications are how a feed calls you back. People who spent one week with their phone's alerts on and another with them off reported more inattention and hyperactivity in the week with alerts on (Kushlev et al., 2016). You don't have to silence everything, though: in another trial, people who turned off every notification felt more anxious and more worried about missing out, while those who got them in three batches a day felt better (Fitz et al., 2019). So switch off the feeds and keep the people. On iPhone: Settings › Notifications › Instagram, then turn off Allow Notifications.

Put a pause before the feed

A few seconds between the urge and the feed is often enough to change your mind. Over six weeks with one sec, an app that makes you take a breath before a chosen app opens, people opened those apps 57% less often, and about a third of the time they changed their mind and closed it. The study had no comparison group, and one of its authors makes the app (Grüning et al., 2023).

iPhone's Screen Time can also set a daily limit for an app (Settings › Screen Time › App Limits), but a limit you can dismiss with a tap is weak. TikTok's own tests found that its 60-minute prompt cut teenagers' use by about a minute and a half a day (NPR, 2024).

Turn the colour off

Grayscale makes the feed less vivid. In one study it cut daily screen time by over half an hour, mostly from social media and browsing (Holte & Ferraro, 2023), and a randomised trial confirmed that it reduces screen time, though not that it improves wellbeing (Zimmermann & Sobolev, 2023). On iPhone: Settings › Accessibility › Display & Text Size › Color Filters, then choose Grayscale. To switch it on and off with a triple-click of the side button, add Color Filters under Settings › Accessibility › Accessibility Shortcut.

Keep the phone out of the bedroom

Charge it in another room and use an alarm clock, so the feed isn't the last thing you see at night or the first thing in the morning.

5. Keep your friends, lose the feed

If you'd rather stay in touch than go dark, keep the parts of social media that are about people:

  • Instagram's Following view shows posts from accounts you follow, newest first. It can't be made the default, though, so the app keeps opening on the regular feed, and Reels and Explore stay a tap away (TechCrunch, 2022).
  • Messages on their own. If what you'd miss is the conversations, keep those and drop the rest. On a computer, instagram.com/direct/inbox takes you straight to your messages.
  • A friends-only app. Unreeled, our free and open-source iPhone app, shows the people you follow and nothing else: their stories, their posts newest first, your messages and your activity. There's no Reels feed, no Explore, no suggested posts and no ads. It's unofficial, and Instagram could restrict an account that uses it, so read about the risk first.
  • Plans. Swap a like for a message, and a message for a call or a coffee. When you meet, put the phones away: people who kept their phones on the table during a meal with friends or family enjoyed it less (Dwyer et al., 2018).

6. Plan for the moments you'd scroll

The urge comes at predictable moments: waking up, waiting, commuting, the end of the day, and whenever you're bored, tired or anxious. Choose something for each before you start, or the time will simply move to other apps. In one large experiment, the time people stopped spending on Instagram mostly went to other apps on their phones (Allcott et al., 2026).

  • A book, a podcast or an audiobook for the commute.
  • A walk, or a few minutes of nothing at all, between tasks.
  • A message or a call to one person, when you want to feel connected.
  • A paper notebook beside the bed, for the thoughts you'd otherwise check your phone about.

Expect the first few days to feel odd. You'll reach for your phone without deciding to. That's the habit, not a need: notice it, and let it pass.

7. Coming back

When your break ends, decide what you want from social media before you reinstall anything:

  • Which people do you want to hear from? Unfollow or mute the rest, starting with the accounts that leave you comparing yourself.
  • Keep the feed's notifications off, and keep the app off your home screen.
  • Give it a time and a place, rather than every gap in your day.
  • Check in with yourself after a week. If the old habit is back, take another break.

A break can change your habits for good. Facebook users who deactivated for four weeks said they used it less in the month afterwards (Allcott et al., 2020), and people paid to cut down kept using less for weeks after the payments stopped (Allcott, Gentzkow & Song, 2022).

Questions

How long should a social media detox be?

A week is enough to notice a difference: in randomised trials, one week off improved wellbeing and eased depression and anxiety. Three to four weeks gives the habit more time to fade. Across trials, longer breaks didn't reliably do better, so pick a length you'll stick to, set an end date, and extend it if you like how it feels.

Does a break from social media really help?

A little, on average. Pooled trials find small improvements in depression, anxiety, stress and wellbeing, though not in loneliness or life satisfaction, and some studies find no benefit at all. Heavy users, and people who often compare themselves with others, seem to gain the most. A break isn't a treatment for depression or anxiety.

Can I keep messaging my friends during a social media break?

Yes. A break is yours to define, and for most people the problem is the feed, not the conversations. Keep your messages, drop the feed, and tell people how to reach you. Cutting yourself off from everyone can even leave you lonelier.

Should I deactivate or delete my Instagram account?

For a break, you don't need to do either. Deleting the app from your phone is enough, and your account stays as it is. If you'd rather disappear for a while, Instagram lets you temporarily deactivate your account, which hides your profile until you log back in. Deleting your account is permanent once Instagram's grace period ends, so save that for when you're sure.

Is a dopamine detox real?

Not literally. Dopamine fasting was popularised in 2019 by Cameron Sepah, a California psychiatrist, as a behavioural technique: taking scheduled breaks from habits you find hard to control, such as checking your phone. He said the name wasn't meant literally. Avoiding stimulation doesn't lower or reset your dopamine, whatever some versions of the idea claim (Grinspoon, 2020). The useful part is the break itself.

How do I use Instagram without Reels?

Instagram has no setting that turns Reels off. Its Following view shows posts from accounts you follow, newest first, but you can't make it the default, and Reels and Explore stay a tap away. Unreeled, our free iPhone app, shows the people you follow and nothing else: no Reels feed, no Explore and no suggested posts. Reels from accounts you follow can be switched on, and then show one at a time, with no feed to swipe. It's unofficial, so read about the risk on our home page first.

A break can help with a habit, not with everything. If you're struggling, talk to someone you trust or to a professional. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14; in the UK and Ireland, Samaritans on 116 123; in the US, call or text 988. Elsewhere, Find A Helpline lists free services by country.
